Part 2: Target Achievement Analysis

I filtered the Sales Target dataset for only the Furniture category and calculated the month-over-month percentage change in target sales using pct_change().

One issue I ran into - the "Month of Order Date" column had a date parsing problem. Excel had stored the year values (18, 19) in the day field instead of the year field. So "Apr-18" was being read as April 18th instead of April 2018. I fixed this programmatically by extracting the day value, adding 2000 to it, and reconstructing the dates properly.

After fixing the dates, here is what I found:

The Furniture sales targets increase in a very smooth, almost straight line from 10,400 (Apr-18) to 11,800 (Mar-19). Each month the target goes up by roughly 100-200 units. The biggest monthly jumps are in Jul-18 (1.89% increase), Nov-18 (1.80%), and Mar-19 (1.72%). But honestly even these "big" jumps are small - less than 2%.

So the targets themselves don't have any major fluctuation. They just go up slowly and steadily.

But the real interesting thing is when you compare targets vs actual sales.

I merged the target data with actual Furniture sales from the orders dataset and here is what happened:

The actual sales are extremely volatile compared to the smooth targets. Actual sales jump from as low as 3,622 (Jun-18) to as high as 15,944 (Jan-19). Meanwhile the targets are sitting in a narrow band of 10,400 to 11,800.

Out of 12 months, the actual sales exceeded the target in only 3 months - Aug-18, Nov-18, and Jan-19. The remaining 9 months fell short. The average achievement rate across all months is just 72.6%, which means the company is consistently missing its Furniture targets by about 27%.

The worst months were Jun-18 where actual was only 34% of target, and Feb-19 where it was only 40%.

My Suggested Strategies:

1. Set targets based on actual performance, not assumptions
Instead of a smooth increasing line, the company should look at last 2-3 months actual sales and set the next target based on that with a small realistic growth of maybe 5-10%. Right now the targets seem disconnected from what is actually happening on the ground.

2. Account for seasonal patterns
The data clearly shows some months are strong (Aug, Nov, Jan) and some are weak (May, Jun, Feb). Instead of a flat increasing line, targets should be higher in historically strong months and lower in weak months. A simple way to do this is to look at last year's same month and adjust from there.

3. Question whether aggressive Furniture targets make sense
From Part 1 we already know Furniture has the lowest profit margin at 1.81%. Pushing high sales targets for a category that barely makes profit doesn't make much business sense. Maybe the focus should be on improving margins first (better deals with suppliers, less discounting) before trying to push volume.

4. Monthly review meetings
Instead of setting all 12 months targets at the beginning of the year, have monthly check-ins where the sales team, designers, and procurement people discuss what is working and what is not. Adjust next month's target based on current market conditions and pipeline.
